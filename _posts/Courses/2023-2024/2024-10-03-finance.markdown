<h3 class="section-title">Vue d'ensemble</h3>

<p>Ce cours fournit aux ingenieurs les fondamentaux de la finance d'entreprise et de l'analyse economique. Il couvre les concepts essentiels pour comprendre la valeur temporelle de l'argent, evaluer des investissements, analyser la performance financiere d'une entreprise, et prendre des decisions eclairees integrant la dimension economique.</p>

<li>Dialoguer efficacement avec les services financiers</li>

<h2>PART C - Aspects Techniques Detailles</h2>

<h3 class="section-title">1. Valeur temporelle de l'argent</h3>

<p><strong>Principe fondamental</strong> :</p>
<p>Un euro aujourd'hui vaut plus qu'un euro demain.</p>

<p>Raisons :</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Inflation</strong> : pouvoir d'achat diminue avec le temps</li>
<li><strong>Cout d'opportunite</strong> : on pourrait investir cet euro et obtenir un rendement</li>
<li><strong>Risque</strong> : l'incertitude augmente avec le temps</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Actualisation</strong> :</p>
<p>Calculer la valeur presente d'un montant futur.</p>

<p>Formule :<br/>
VA = VF / (1 + r)^n</p>

<p>avec :</p>
<ul>
<li>VA : valeur actuelle (presente)</li>
<li>VF : valeur future</li>
<li>r : taux d'actualisation (taux de rendement requis)</li>
<li>n : nombre de periodes (annees)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
Quelle est la valeur actuelle de 1000&euro; recus dans 5 ans, avec un taux d'actualisation de 8% ?</p>

<p>VA = 1000 / (1.08)^5 = 1000 / 1.469 = 681&euro;</p>

<p><strong>Capitalisation</strong> :</p>
<p>Calculer la valeur future d'un montant present.</p>

<p>Formule :<br/>
VF = VA &times; (1 + r)^n</p>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
Combien vaudront 1000&euro; investis aujourd'hui dans 5 ans a 8% ?</p>

<p>VF = 1000 &times; (1.08)^5 = 1000 &times; 1.469 = 1469&euro;</p>

<p><strong>Annuites</strong> :</p>
<p>Serie de flux constants (ex : mensualites de pret, rente).</p>

<p>Valeur actuelle d'une annuite :<br/>
VA = A &times; [(1 - (1+r)^-n) / r]</p>

<p>avec A : montant de l'annuite.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">2. Documents comptables</h3>

<p><strong>Bilan</strong> :</p>
<p>Photographie du patrimoine de l'entreprise a un instant donne.</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>ACTIF (Emplois)</th><th>PASSIF (Ressources)</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Actif immobilise (long terme)</td><td>Capitaux propres</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Immobilisations incorporelles</td><td>- Capital social</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Immobilisations corporelles</td><td>- Reserves</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Immobilisations financieres</td><td>- Resultat net</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Actif circulant (court terme)</td><td>Dettes</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Stocks</td><td>- Dettes financieres</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Creances clients</td><td>- Dettes fournisseurs</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Tresorerie</td><td>- Dettes fiscales</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Equation fondamentale :<br/>
<strong>ACTIF = PASSIF</strong></p>

<p><strong>Compte de resultat</strong> :</p>
<p>Film de l'activite sur une periode (generalement un an).</p>

<p>Structure simplifiee :</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chiffre d'affaires</strong> (ventes)</li>
<li>- Cout des ventes</li>
<li>= <strong>Marge commerciale</strong></li>
<li>+ Production de l'exercice</li>
<li>- Consommations intermediaires</li>
<li>= <strong>Valeur ajoutee (VA)</strong></li>
<li>+ Subventions d'exploitation</li>
<li>- Impots et taxes</li>
<li>- Charges de personnel</li>
<li>= <strong>Excedent Brut d'Exploitation (EBE)</strong></li>
<li>+ Autres produits d'exploitation</li>
<li>- Autres charges d'exploitation</li>
<li>- Dotations aux amortissements</li>
<li>= <strong>Resultat d'exploitation</strong></li>
<li>+ Produits financiers</li>
<li>- Charges financieres</li>
<li>= <strong>Resultat Courant Avant Impots (RCAI)</strong></li>
<li>+ Resultat exceptionnel</li>
<li>- Participation des salaries</li>
<li>- Impots sur les benefices</li>
<li>= <strong>Resultat net</strong></li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Script Python Finance.py</strong> :</p>
<p>Le script automatise le calcul de tous ces indicateurs :</p>
<ul>
<li>Fonctions pour chaque etape (marge commerciale, VA, EBE, resultat d'exploitation, RCAI, resultat net)</li>
<li>Saisie interactive des donnees</li>
<li>Affichage des resultats intermediaires et finaux</li>
</ul>

<h3 class="section-title">3. Analyse financiere et ratios</h3>

<p><strong>Ratios de rentabilite</strong> :</p>

<p><strong>Rentabilite des Actifs (ROA)</strong> :<br/>
ROA = Resultat Net / Actif Total &times; 100%</p>
<p>Mesure l'efficacite d'utilisation des actifs.</p>

<p><strong>Rentabilite des Capitaux Propres (ROE)</strong> :<br/>
ROE = Resultat Net / Capitaux Propres &times; 100%</p>
<p>Mesure le rendement pour les actionnaires.</p>

<p><strong>Marge nette</strong> :<br/>
Marge nette = Resultat Net / Chiffre d'Affaires &times; 100%</p>

<p><strong>Ratios de liquidite</strong> :</p>

<p><strong>Liquidite generale</strong> :<br/>
Ratio = Actif Circulant / Passif Circulant</p>
<p>Si &gt; 1 : entreprise peut payer ses dettes court terme.</p>

<p><strong>Liquidite immediate (quick ratio)</strong> :<br/>
Ratio = (Actif Circulant - Stocks) / Passif Circulant</p>
<p>Plus conservateur (stocks parfois difficiles a vendre rapidement).</p>

<p><strong>Ratios d'endettement</strong> :</p>

<p><strong>Taux d'endettement</strong> :<br/>
Ratio = Dettes Totales / Capitaux Propres</p>
<p>Mesure le levier financier.</p>

<p><strong>Ratio de couverture des interets</strong> :<br/>
Ratio = EBIT / Charges d'Interets</p>
<p>Capacite a payer les interets de la dette.</p>

<p><strong>Ratios d'activite</strong> :</p>

<p><strong>Rotation des stocks</strong> :<br/>
Ratio = Cout des Ventes / Stock Moyen</p>
<p>Nombre de fois ou le stock est renouvele dans l'annee.</p>

<p><strong>Delai de paiement clients</strong> :<br/>
Delai = (Creances Clients / CA TTC) &times; 365 jours</p>

<p><strong>Delai de paiement fournisseurs</strong> :<br/>
Delai = (Dettes Fournisseurs / Achats TTC) &times; 365 jours</p>

<h3 class="section-title">4. Evaluation de projets d'investissement</h3>

<p><strong>Valeur Actuelle Nette (VAN)</strong> :</p>
<p>Indicateur principal pour evaluer la rentabilite d'un investissement.</p>

<p>Formule :<br/>
VAN = -I0 + somme(CFt / (1+r)^t)</p>

<p>avec :</p>
<ul>
<li>I0 : investissement initial</li>
<li>CFt : cash-flow a la periode t</li>
<li>r : taux d'actualisation</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Regle de decision</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>VAN &gt; 0 : projet rentable (cree de la valeur)</li>
<li>VAN &lt; 0 : projet non rentable (detruit de la valeur)</li>
<li>VAN = 0 : projet neutre</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
Investissement de 10000&euro;, cash-flows annuels de 3000&euro; pendant 5 ans, taux d'actualisation 10%.</p>

<p>VAN = -10000 + 3000/(1.1) + 3000/(1.1)^2 + 3000/(1.1)^3 + 3000/(1.1)^4 + 3000/(1.1)^5<br/>
VAN = -10000 + 2727 + 2479 + 2254 + 2049 + 1863 = 1372&euro;</p>

<p>VAN positive &rarr; projet rentable.</p>

<p><strong>Taux de Rentabilite Interne (TRI)</strong> :</p>
<p>Taux d'actualisation qui annule la VAN.</p>

<p>Equation :<br/>
-I0 + somme(CFt / (1+TRI)^t) = 0</p>

<p><strong>Regle de decision</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>TRI &gt; taux de rendement requis : accepter projet</li>
<li>TRI &lt; taux de rendement requis : rejeter projet</li>
</ul>

<p>Interpretation : rendement effectif du projet.</p>

<p><strong>Methodes de calcul</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Iteration (essai-erreur)</li>
<li>Fonction TRI dans Excel</li>
<li>Resolution numerique (Newton-Raphson)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Delai de recuperation (Payback)</strong> :</p>
<p>Temps necessaire pour recuperer l'investissement initial.</p>

<p><strong>Avantages</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Simple et intuitif</li>
<li>Mesure du risque (plus court = moins risque)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Inconvenients</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Ignore valeur temporelle de l'argent (version simple)</li>
<li>Ignore cash-flows apres recuperation</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Indice de Profitabilite (IP)</strong> :</p>
<p>IP = VAN / I0</p>
<p>ou</p>
<p>IP = Somme(VA des CF) / I0</p>
<p>Utile pour comparer des projets de tailles differentes.</p>
<p>Si IP &gt; 1 : projet rentable.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">5. Cout du capital</h3>

<p><strong>Cout des fonds propres</strong> :</p>
<p>Rendement exige par les actionnaires.</p>

<p><strong>Modele CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)</strong> :</p>
<p>re = rf + &beta; &times; (rm - rf)</p>

<p>avec :</p>
<ul>
<li>re : cout des fonds propres</li>
<li>rf : taux sans risque (obligations d'Etat)</li>
<li>&beta; : coefficient beta (risque systematique de l'action)</li>
<li>rm : rendement espere du marche</li>
<li>(rm - rf) : prime de risque de marche</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
rf = 3%, &beta; = 1.2, rm = 10%</p>
<p>re = 3% + 1.2 &times; (10% - 3%) = 3% + 8.4% = 11.4%</p>

<p><strong>Cout de la dette</strong> :</p>
<p>rd = taux d'interet &times; (1 - taux d'imposition)</p>
<p>L'economie d'impot (tax shield) reduit le cout effectif.</p>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
Taux d'interet 5%, taux d'imposition 30%</p>
<p>rd = 5% &times; (1 - 0.30) = 3.5%</p>

<p><strong>Cout Moyen Pondere du Capital (CMPC ou WACC)</strong> :</p>
<p>WACC = (E/V) &times; re + (D/V) &times; rd &times; (1 - T)</p>

<p>avec :</p>
<ul>
<li>E : valeur des fonds propres (equity)</li>
<li>D : valeur de la dette (debt)</li>
<li>V = E + D : valeur totale de l'entreprise</li>
<li>T : taux d'imposition</li>
</ul>

<p>Le WACC represente le cout moyen de financement de l'entreprise et sert de taux d'actualisation pour evaluer les projets.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">6. Structure financiere</h3>

<p><strong>Theoreme de Modigliani-Miller</strong> :</p>

<p><strong>Proposition I (sans impots)</strong> :<br/>
La valeur d'une entreprise est independante de sa structure de capital (dette vs fonds propres).</p>

<p><strong>Proposition II (avec impots)</strong> :<br/>
L'endettement cree de la valeur grace a l'economie d'impot sur les interets.</p>

<p>Valeur entreprise endettee = Valeur entreprise non endettee + T &times; Dette</p>

<p><strong>Effet de levier</strong> :</p>
<p>L'endettement amplifie les rendements pour les actionnaires (positifs ou negatifs).</p>

<p><strong>Avantages de la dette</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Cout inferieur aux fonds propres</li>
<li>Economie d'impot (interets deductibles)</li>
<li>Discipline de gestion (obligation de payer)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Inconvenients de la dette</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Risque de faillite si incapacite a rembourser</li>
<li>Couts de detresse financiere</li>
<li>Perte de flexibilite</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Structure optimale</strong> :</p>
<p>Compromis entre avantages fiscaux et couts de detresse.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">7. Flux de tresorerie (Cash-Flow)</h3>

<p><strong>Cash-Flow Operationnel (CFO)</strong> :</p>
<p>Tresorerie generee par l'activite.</p>

<p>CFO = EBIT &times; (1 - T) + Amortissements - Variation BFR</p>

<p>avec :</p>
<ul>
<li>EBIT : resultat d'exploitation</li>
<li>T : taux d'imposition</li>
<li>BFR : Besoin en Fonds de Roulement</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Free Cash-Flow (FCF)</strong> :</p>
<p>Tresorerie disponible apres financement des investissements.</p>

<p>FCF = CFO - CAPEX</p>
<p>avec CAPEX : depenses d'investissement.</p>

<p>Le FCF sert a remunerer actionnaires (dividendes) et creanciers (remboursement dette).</p>

<p><strong>Besoin en Fonds de Roulement (BFR)</strong> :</p>
<p>BFR = Stocks + Creances Clients - Dettes Fournisseurs</p>
<p>Une augmentation du BFR consomme de la tresorerie (immobilisation dans le cycle d'exploitation).</p>

<h3 class="section-title">8. Valorisation d'entreprise</h3>

<p><strong>Methode des flux actualises (DCF)</strong> :</p>
<p>Valeur entreprise = Somme(FCF actualises) + Valeur terminale</p>
<p>Valeur terminale = FCF(n+1) / (WACC - g)</p>
<p>avec g : taux de croissance perpetuelle (typiquement 2-3%).</p>

<p><strong>Methode des comparables</strong> :</p>
<p>Valorisation relative par rapport a entreprises similaires.</p>

<p><strong>Multiples courants</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>P/E (Price/Earnings) : Capitalisation / Resultat Net</li>
<li>EV/EBITDA : Valeur d'Entreprise / EBITDA</li>
<li>P/B (Price/Book) : Capitalisation / Capitaux Propres</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Exemple</strong> :<br/>
Entreprises du secteur : P/E moyen = 15<br/>
Notre entreprise : Resultat Net = 5M&euro;<br/>
Valorisation = 15 &times; 5M = 75M&euro;</p>

<p><strong>Methode patrimoniale</strong> :</p>
<p>Valeur = Actifs - Dettes</p>
<p>Approche comptable, pertinente pour holdings ou liquidation.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">9. Marches financiers</h3>

<p><strong>Marche primaire</strong> :</p>
<p>Emission de nouveaux titres (actions, obligations).</p>
<ul>
<li>Introduction en bourse (IPO)</li>
<li>Augmentation de capital</li>
<li>Emission obligataire</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Marche secondaire</strong> :</p>
<p>Echange de titres existants (bourses : Euronext, NYSE, NASDAQ).</p>

<p><strong>Actions</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Propriete d'une fraction de l'entreprise</li>
<li>Droit aux dividendes</li>
<li>Droit de vote (assemblees generales)</li>
<li>Rendement : dividendes + plus-values</li>
<li>Risque eleve</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Obligations</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Titre de creance (pret a l'entreprise ou Etat)</li>
<li>Paiement d'interets periodiques (coupons)</li>
<li>Remboursement du principal a echeance</li>
<li>Risque plus faible que actions</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Produits derives</strong> :</p>
<ul>
<li>Options (call : droit d'acheter, put : droit de vendre)</li>
<li>Futures et forwards (contrats a terme)</li>
<li>Swaps (echange de flux financiers)</li>
</ul>

<p>Utilises pour couverture (hedging) ou speculation.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">10. Gestion du risque financier</h3>

<p><strong>Types de risques</strong> :</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Risque</th><th>Description</th><th>Exemple</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Marche</td><td>Fluctuations cours (actions, devises, matieres premieres)</td><td>Chute du cours de l'action</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Credit</td><td>Defaut de paiement d'un debiteur</td><td>Client ne paie pas</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Liquidite</td><td>Incapacite a vendre rapidement un actif</td><td>Actif immobilier difficilement vendable</td></tr>

<h2>PART C - Detailed Technical Aspects</h2>

<h3 class="section-title">1. Time Value of Money</h3>

<p><strong>Fundamental principle</strong>:</p>
<p>One euro today is worth more than one euro tomorrow.</p>

<p>Reasons:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Inflation</strong>: purchasing power decreases over time</li>
<li><strong>Opportunity cost</strong>: one could invest that euro and earn a return</li>
<li><strong>Risk</strong>: uncertainty increases over time</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Discounting</strong>:</p>
<p>Calculating the present value of a future amount.</p>

<p>Formula:<br/>
PV = FV / (1 + r)^n</p>

<p>where:</p>
<ul>
<li>PV: present value</li>
<li>FV: future value</li>
<li>r: discount rate (required rate of return)</li>
<li>n: number of periods (years)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
What is the present value of &euro;1,000 received in 5 years, with a discount rate of 8%?</p>

<p>PV = 1000 / (1.08)^5 = 1000 / 1.469 = &euro;681</p>

<p><strong>Compounding</strong>:</p>
<p>Calculating the future value of a present amount.</p>

<p>Formula:<br/>
FV = PV &times; (1 + r)^n</p>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
How much will &euro;1,000 invested today be worth in 5 years at 8%?</p>

<p>FV = 1000 &times; (1.08)^5 = 1000 &times; 1.469 = &euro;1,469</p>

<p><strong>Annuities</strong>:</p>
<p>Series of constant cash flows (e.g., loan installments, pensions).</p>

<p>Present value of an annuity:<br/>
PV = A &times; [(1 - (1+r)^-n) / r]</p>

<p>where A: annuity amount.</p>

<p><strong>Balance Sheet</strong>:</p>
<p>A snapshot of the company's assets and liabilities at a given point in time.</p>

  <tr><td>Non-current assets (long-term)</td><td>Shareholders' equity</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Intangible assets</td><td>- Share capital</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Tangible assets</td><td>- Reserves</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Financial assets</td><td>- Net income</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Current assets (short-term)</td><td>Liabilities</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Inventories</td><td>- Financial liabilities</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Trade receivables</td><td>- Trade payables</td></tr>
  <tr><td>- Cash</td><td>- Tax liabilities</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Fundamental equation:<br/>
<strong>ASSETS = LIABILITIES + EQUITY</strong></p>

<p><strong>Income Statement</strong>:</p>
<p>A record of business activity over a period (typically one year).</p>

<p>Simplified structure:</p>

<h3 class="section-title">3. Financial Analysis and Ratios</h3>

<p><strong>Profitability ratios</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Return on Assets (ROA)</strong>:<br/>
ROA = Net Income / Total Assets &times; 100%</p>
<p>Measures the efficiency of asset utilization.</p>

<p><strong>Return on Equity (ROE)</strong>:<br/>
ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity &times; 100%</p>
<p>Measures the return for shareholders.</p>

<p><strong>Net margin</strong>:<br/>
Net margin = Net Income / Revenue &times; 100%</p>

<p><strong>Liquidity ratios</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Current ratio</strong>:<br/>
Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities</p>
<p>If &gt; 1: the company can pay its short-term debts.</p>

<p><strong>Quick ratio</strong>:<br/>
Ratio = (Current Assets - Inventories) / Current Liabilities</p>
<p>More conservative (inventories can sometimes be difficult to sell quickly).</p>

<p><strong>Leverage ratios</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Debt-to-equity ratio</strong>:<br/>
Ratio = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity</p>
<p>Measures financial leverage.</p>

<p><strong>Interest coverage ratio</strong>:<br/>
Ratio = EBIT / Interest Expenses</p>
<p>Ability to pay interest on debt.</p>

<p><strong>Activity ratios</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Inventory turnover</strong>:<br/>
Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory</p>
<p>Number of times inventory is renewed during the year.</p>

<p><strong>Days sales outstanding</strong>:<br/>
DSO = (Trade Receivables / Revenue incl. tax) &times; 365 days</p>

<p><strong>Days payable outstanding</strong>:<br/>
DPO = (Trade Payables / Purchases incl. tax) &times; 365 days</p>

<h3 class="section-title">4. Investment Project Evaluation</h3>

<p><strong>Net Present Value (NPV)</strong>:</p>
<p>The primary indicator for evaluating investment profitability.</p>

<p>Formula:<br/>
NPV = -I0 + sum(CFt / (1+r)^t)</p>

<p>where:</p>
<ul>
<li>I0: initial investment</li>
<li>CFt: cash flow at period t</li>
<li>r: discount rate</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Decision rule</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>NPV &gt; 0: profitable project (creates value)</li>
<li>NPV &lt; 0: unprofitable project (destroys value)</li>
<li>NPV = 0: neutral project</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
Investment of &euro;10,000, annual cash flows of &euro;3,000 for 5 years, discount rate 10%.</p>

<p>NPV = -10000 + 3000/(1.1) + 3000/(1.1)^2 + 3000/(1.1)^3 + 3000/(1.1)^4 + 3000/(1.1)^5<br/>
NPV = -10000 + 2727 + 2479 + 2254 + 2049 + 1863 = &euro;1,372</p>

<p>Positive NPV &rarr; profitable project.</p>

<p><strong>Internal Rate of Return (IRR)</strong>:</p>
<p>The discount rate that makes the NPV equal to zero.</p>

<p>Equation:<br/>
-I0 + sum(CFt / (1+IRR)^t) = 0</p>

<p><strong>Decision rule</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>IRR &gt; required rate of return: accept the project</li>
<li>IRR &lt; required rate of return: reject the project</li>
</ul>

<p>Interpretation: effective return of the project.</p>

<p><strong>Calculation methods</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Iteration (trial and error)</li>
<li>IRR function in Excel</li>
<li>Numerical resolution (Newton-Raphson)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Payback Period</strong>:</p>
<p>Time required to recover the initial investment.</p>

<p><strong>Advantages</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Simple and intuitive</li>
<li>Risk measure (shorter = less risky)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ignores time value of money (simple version)</li>
<li>Ignores cash flows after recovery</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Profitability Index (PI)</strong>:</p>
<p>PI = NPV / I0</p>
<p>or</p>
<p>PI = Sum(PV of CFs) / I0</p>
<p>Useful for comparing projects of different sizes.</p>
<p>If PI &gt; 1: profitable project.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">5. Cost of Capital</h3>

<p><strong>Cost of equity</strong>:</p>
<p>Return required by shareholders.</p>

<p><strong>CAPM Model (Capital Asset Pricing Model)</strong>:</p>
<p>re = rf + &beta; &times; (rm - rf)</p>

<p>where:</p>
<ul>
<li>re: cost of equity</li>
<li>rf: risk-free rate (government bonds)</li>
<li>&beta;: beta coefficient (systematic risk of the stock)</li>
<li>rm: expected market return</li>
<li>(rm - rf): market risk premium</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
rf = 3%, &beta; = 1.2, rm = 10%</p>
<p>re = 3% + 1.2 &times; (10% - 3%) = 3% + 8.4% = 11.4%</p>

<p><strong>Cost of debt</strong>:</p>
<p>rd = interest rate &times; (1 - tax rate)</p>
<p>The tax shield reduces the effective cost.</p>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
Interest rate 5%, tax rate 30%</p>
<p>rd = 5% &times; (1 - 0.30) = 3.5%</p>

<p><strong>Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)</strong>:</p>
<p>WACC = (E/V) &times; re + (D/V) &times; rd &times; (1 - T)</p>

<p>where:</p>
<ul>
<li>E: value of equity</li>
<li>D: value of debt</li>
<li>V = E + D: total enterprise value</li>
<li>T: tax rate</li>
</ul>

<p>The WACC represents the company's average cost of financing and serves as the discount rate for evaluating projects.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">6. Financial Structure</h3>

<p><strong>Modigliani-Miller Theorem</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Proposition I (without taxes)</strong>:<br/>
The value of a company is independent of its capital structure (debt vs. equity).</p>

<p><strong>Proposition II (with taxes)</strong>:<br/>
Debt creates value through the tax shield on interest payments.</p>

<p>Levered firm value = Unlevered firm value + T &times; Debt</p>

<p><strong>Leverage effect</strong>:</p>
<p>Debt amplifies returns for shareholders (both positive and negative).</p>

<p><strong>Advantages of debt</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Lower cost than equity</li>
<li>Tax shield (deductible interest)</li>
<li>Management discipline (obligation to pay)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Disadvantages of debt</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bankruptcy risk if unable to repay</li>
<li>Financial distress costs</li>
<li>Loss of flexibility</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Optimal structure</strong>:</p>
<p>Trade-off between tax advantages and distress costs.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">7. Cash Flow</h3>

<p><strong>Operating Cash Flow (OCF)</strong>:</p>
<p>Cash generated by operations.</p>

<p>OCF = EBIT &times; (1 - T) + Depreciation - Change in Working Capital</p>

<p>where:</p>
<ul>
<li>EBIT: operating income</li>
<li>T: tax rate</li>
<li>Working Capital: net working capital requirements</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Free Cash Flow (FCF)</strong>:</p>
<p>Cash available after financing investments.</p>

<p>FCF = OCF - CAPEX</p>
<p>where CAPEX: capital expenditures.</p>

<p>FCF is used to pay shareholders (dividends) and creditors (debt repayment).</p>

<p><strong>Working Capital Requirements</strong>:</p>
<p>WCR = Inventories + Trade Receivables - Trade Payables</p>
<p>An increase in WCR consumes cash (tied up in the operating cycle).</p>

<h3 class="section-title">8. Company Valuation</h3>

<p><strong>Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) method</strong>:</p>
<p>Enterprise value = Sum(Discounted FCFs) + Terminal Value</p>
<p>Terminal Value = FCF(n+1) / (WACC - g)</p>
<p>where g: perpetual growth rate (typically 2-3%).</p>

<p><strong>Comparable method</strong>:</p>
<p>Relative valuation compared to similar companies.</p>

<p><strong>Common multiples</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>P/E (Price/Earnings): Market Cap / Net Income</li>
<li>EV/EBITDA: Enterprise Value / EBITDA</li>
<li>P/B (Price/Book): Market Cap / Shareholders' Equity</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:<br/>
Industry peers: average P/E = 15<br/>
Our company: Net Income = &euro;5M<br/>
Valuation = 15 &times; 5M = &euro;75M</p>

<p><strong>Asset-based method</strong>:</p>
<p>Value = Assets - Liabilities</p>
<p>Accounting approach, relevant for holding companies or liquidation.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">9. Financial Markets</h3>

<p><strong>Primary market</strong>:</p>
<p>Issuance of new securities (stocks, bonds).</p>
<ul>
<li>Initial Public Offering (IPO)</li>
<li>Capital increase</li>
<li>Bond issuance</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Secondary market</strong>:</p>
<p>Trading of existing securities (exchanges: Euronext, NYSE, NASDAQ).</p>

<p><strong>Stocks</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ownership of a fraction of the company</li>
<li>Right to dividends</li>
<li>Voting rights (general assemblies)</li>
<li>Return: dividends + capital gains</li>
<li>High risk</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Bonds</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Debt instrument (loan to a company or government)</li>
<li>Periodic interest payments (coupons)</li>
<li>Principal repayment at maturity</li>
<li>Lower risk than stocks</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Derivatives</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Options (call: right to buy, put: right to sell)</li>
<li>Futures and forwards (forward contracts)</li>
<li>Swaps (exchange of financial flows)</li>
</ul>

<p>Used for hedging or speculation.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">10. Financial Risk Management</h3>

<p><strong>Types of risks</strong>:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Risk</th><th>Description</th><th>Example</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Market</td><td>Price fluctuations (stocks, currencies, commodities)</td><td>Stock price crash</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Credit</td><td>Default by a debtor</td><td>Customer does not pay</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Liquidity</td><td>Inability to sell an asset quickly</td><td>Real estate asset hard to sell</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Operational</td><td>Internal failures (processes, systems, human)</td><td>IT failure, fraud</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Interest rate</td><td>Rate changes impacting debt</td><td>Rate increase &rarr; debt cost rises</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Currency</td><td>Exchange rate fluctuations</td><td>Exports in USD, &euro; appreciates &rarr; loss</td></tr>
</table>

<p><strong>Hedging instruments</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Forwards/Futures</strong>: lock in a future price</li>
<li><strong>Options</strong>: right (not obligation) to buy/sell at a fixed price</li>
<li><strong>Swaps</strong>: exchange of flows (fixed rate vs. variable rate)</li>
</ul>

<h3 class="section-title">11. Financing Decisions</h3>

<p><strong>Sources of financing</strong>:</p>

<p><strong>Equity</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Self-financing (reinvested profits)</li>
<li>Capital increase (share issuance)</li>
<li>Venture capital, business angels (startups)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Debt</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Bank loans</li>
<li>Bonds (corporate bonds)</li>
<li>Leasing</li>
<li>Factoring (assignment of trade receivables)</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Selection criteria</strong>:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Criterion</th><th>Equity</th><th>Debt</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Cost</td><td>High (shareholder expectations)</td><td>Lower (interest)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Risk</td><td>No repayment obligation</td><td>Payment obligation</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Control</td><td>Capital dilution</td><td>No dilution</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Flexibility</td><td>Flexible</td><td>Constraints (covenants)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Tax treatment</td><td>No tax advantage</td><td>Deductible interest</td></tr>
</table>

<h3 class="section-title">12. Sensitivity Analysis</h3>

<p><strong>Objective</strong>:</p>
<p>Measure the impact of parameter variations on NPV (or IRR).</p>

<p><strong>Parameters tested</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Revenue (volume, price)</li>
<li>Costs (raw materials, personnel)</li>
<li>Discount rate</li>
<li>Initial investment</li>
<li>Project duration</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Example</strong>:</p>
<p>Project with base NPV = &euro;1,000.</p>
<p>Revenue variation of &plusmn;10%:</p>
<ul>
<li>Revenue +10% &rarr; NPV = &euro;1,500</li>
<li>Revenue -10% &rarr; NPV = &euro;500</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Scenario analysis</strong>:</p>
<p>Define several complete scenarios:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Optimistic</strong>: favorable assumptions</li>
<li><strong>Base</strong>: realistic assumptions</li>
<li><strong>Pessimistic</strong>: unfavorable assumptions</li>
</ul>

<p>Calculate NPV for each scenario and assess risk.</p>

<p><strong>Monte Carlo Simulation</strong>:</p>
<p>Randomly generate thousands of scenarios to obtain a probability distribution of NPV.</p>
<p>Allows estimating the probability that NPV is positive.</p>

<h2>PART D - Reflective Analysis and Perspectives</h2>

<h3 class="section-title">Skills Acquired</h3>

<p><strong>Mastery of financial concepts</strong>:</p>
<p>Ability to understand and apply fundamental principles: time value, discounting, investment evaluation.</p>

<p><strong>Financial statement analysis</strong>:</p>
<p>Ability to read and interpret balance sheets and income statements, calculate and analyze financial ratios.</p>

<p><strong>Project evaluation</strong>:</p>
<p>Competence in evaluating investment profitability with NPV, IRR, and analyzing sensitivity to assumptions.</p>

<p><strong>Economic vision</strong>:</p>
<p>Development of an understanding of value creation mechanisms and a company's financial challenges.</p>

<p><strong>Communication with financial professionals</strong>:</p>
<p>Ability to communicate effectively with financial management, investors, and banks.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">Key Takeaways</h3>

<p><strong>1. Time value = foundation</strong>:</p>
<p>Every financial decision rests on the principle that one euro today is worth more than one euro tomorrow. Discounting follows from this.</p>

<p><strong>2. NPV = key indicator</strong>:</p>
<p>NPV is the primary criterion for evaluating an investment. It directly measures value creation (or destruction).</p>

<p><strong>3. Risk and return are linked</strong>:</p>
<p>The higher the risk, the higher the required return (risk premium). An inevitable trade-off.</p>

<p><strong>4. Cash is king</strong>:</p>
<p>Cash flow is more important than accounting profit. A company can be profitable on paper but bankrupt if it has no cash.</p>

<p><strong>5. No magic formula</strong>:</p>
<p>Financial models rest on assumptions. Judgment and experience are essential for interpreting results.</p>

<h3 class="section-title">Feedback</h3>

<p><strong>Usefulness for engineers</strong>:</p>
<p>This course, although non-technical, is fundamental. Engineers must economically justify their technical choices (investments, R&amp;D, new products).</p>

<p><strong>Practice with tools</strong>:</p>
<p>Using Excel and the Python script made calculations concrete. Automating financial analyses is a valuable skill.</p>

<p><strong>Hidden complexity</strong>:</p>
<p>The formulas seem simple, but their application requires judgment (choosing the discount rate, estimating future cash flows).</p>

<p><strong>Connection with reality</strong>:</p>

<h3 class="section-title">Tips for Success</h3>

<p><strong>1. Understand before calculating</strong>:</p>
<p>Know where the formulas come from and what they represent. Do not apply them mechanically.</p>

<p><strong>2. Practice with Excel</strong>:</p>
<p>Build your own financial models. Master NPV(), IRR() functions, amortization tables.</p>

<p><strong>3. Think in cash flow</strong>:</p>
<p>Always reason in terms of cash flow, not accounting profit. Cash is the economic reality.</p>

<p><strong>4. Analyze sensitivity</strong>:</p>
<p>A single NPV calculation is not enough. Test different scenarios to assess risk.</p>

<p><strong>5. Develop intuition</strong>:</p>
<p>With practice, develop a sense of orders of magnitude (typical discount rates, normal ratios by sector).</p>

<p><strong>6. Follow economic news</strong>:</p>
<p>Read financial press (Les Echos, Financial Times) to understand real-world applications of concepts.</p>
